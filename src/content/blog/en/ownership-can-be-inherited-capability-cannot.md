<p>Ownership of a family business can be passed on to the next generation. But the capability to understand capital, make decisions and take responsibility for their consequences is not passed on with the ownership stakes. It develops through experience, knowledge and the gradual taking on of responsibility.</p>

<p>Imagine a founder who spent thirty years building a successful company. They have been through financial crises, made hard decisions, negotiated with banks and invested their own capital when no one else was willing to take the risk.</p>

<p>Today the company is worth several tens of millions of euros.</p>

<p>They have three children. One works in the company, another has built a successful career outside the family business, and the third is still finding their professional path.</p>

<p>The founder wants to be fair. They believe all the children should inherit equal ownership stakes.</p>

<p>At first glance, the decision is understandable. All the children are equally theirs, and they want to secure a future for each of them.</p>

<p>But there is a question that often goes unasked at this point.</p>

<p><strong>Are all the children equally ready to be owners?</strong></p>

<p>I do not mean their education, business experience or ability to run a company. I mean something more fundamental: do they understand what it actually means to own capital that took decades to create, and where decisions will affect the future of employees, the family and the generations to come?</p>

<p>Because ownership is not just a right to value someone else created.</p>

<p>It is also responsibility for what happens to that value.</p>

<h2>Inheritance transfers rights, but not capabilities</h2>

<p>When a founder transfers ownership stakes to the children, the legal relationship can change almost overnight.</p>

<p>A child becomes an owner. They acquire the right to take part in certain decisions, the right to information, and the economic rights attached to their stake.</p>

<p>But none of that means they have at the same time acquired the capability to understand those rights and their consequences.</p>

<p>An heir can own a third of a company worth thirty million euros without ever having had to decide whether profit should be reinvested or paid out, how much debt the company may take on, or what risk the owners should accept for the sake of future growth.</p>

<p>They can have a vote on important questions without ever having learned to distinguish personal wishes from the long-term interests of the company.</p>

<p>They can expect a dividend without understanding why reported profit is not the same as available cash.</p>

<p>This does not mean they are a bad person or that they do not deserve the inheritance.</p>

<p>It only means that <strong>the right to ownership and the capability to make responsible ownership decisions are not the same thing</strong>.</p>

<p>Founders often fail to notice this difference because their own experience was completely different.</p>

<p>They did not first receive capital and then learn how to manage it. In most cases they created the capital precisely through a process of learning, mistakes, risk and responsibility.</p>

<p>Every important decision had consequences they felt personally.</p>

<p>The next generation often enters the process in reverse. The capital already exists, and they have yet to learn how to carry the responsibility for it.</p>

<p>That is one of the greatest differences between the generation that created the wealth and the generation that inherits it.</p>

<h2>A good heir does not have to be a good managing director</h2>

<p>In family businesses it is often assumed that the child who inherits the ownership will one day take over the management as well.</p>

<p>But ownership and management are two different roles.</p>

<p>A managing director has to know how to lead people, develop the business, make operational decisions and answer for results. Their capability shows in how well the company is managed.</p>

<p>An owner has a different responsibility.</p>

<p>They have to understand what kind of company they want to own, how much risk they are willing to accept, what they expect from management and how they will make the decisions that affect the long-term value of the capital.</p>

<p>They do not have to run the business themselves, but they must be able to judge the people the management has been entrusted to.</p>

<p>They do not have to be a financial expert, but they must understand enough to ask the right questions.</p>

<p>They do not have to take part in day-to-day decisions, but they must understand the line between ownership oversight and operational management.</p>

<p>Imagine a family in which one child runs the company while the other two are co-owners who work outside it.</p>

<p>The child who runs the company may argue for major investment and for retaining profits. The other owners may want regular dividends, because they draw no salary from the family business.</p>

<p>Neither position is wrong in itself.</p>

<p>The problem arises if the family members have never learned to talk about differing interests from the perspective of responsible ownership.</p>

<p>Then a business decision easily turns into a family conflict.</p>

<p>One brother may believe the other wants to drain money out of the company. The other may feel that the brother running the business treats their shared assets as if they were exclusively his own.</p>

<p>There does not have to be bad intent behind such conflicts.</p>

<p>Sometimes the problem is simply that the family has never developed a shared understanding of ownership responsibility.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">A family business does not only need a capable managing director. It also needs capable owners.</div>

<h2>Capital can be inherited before the heir is ready</h2>

<p>Parents naturally want to secure a better life for their children.</p>

<p>That is exactly why many founders worked for decades, made sacrifices and took risks. They wanted to create a security they themselves may never have had.</p>

<p>But financial security and the capability to manage wealth are not the same.</p>

<p>A large inheritance can open opportunities for the next generation that the parents never had. It can make education, entrepreneurship, personal freedom and long-term independence possible.</p>

<p>But it can also create new problems if the capital arrives without an understanding of its value, its limits and the responsibility it carries.</p>

<p>Someone who has never had to make hard financial decisions may struggle to understand why not every investment opportunity is a good one.</p>

<p>Someone who has never felt the consequences of business risk may underestimate the possibility of loss.</p>

<p>Someone used to the family capital always being there may not understand that even substantial wealth can gradually be spent or lost.</p>

<p>And it is not the amount of money inherited that is decisive.</p>

<p>What is decisive is the relationship to capital developed before the inheritance.</p>

<p>A founder can transfer millions of euros, but they cannot automatically transfer their own discipline, experience, patience and ability to make decisions under pressure.</p>

<p>Those capabilities took years to form.</p>

<p>That is why preparing the next generation cannot begin only when the moment of the formal transfer of ownership arrives.</p>

<p>It has to come before the inheritance.</p>

<h2>From heir to responsible owner</h2>

<p>How, then, does a future owner's capability develop?</p>

<p>There is no single educational programme, degree or number of years of work experience that can guarantee readiness.</p>

<p>The capability for ownership develops gradually, through understanding the business, financial literacy, taking part in conversations about capital and the experience of making decisions.</p>

<p>It matters that a future owner understands where the company's value comes from.</p>

<p>What makes it competitive? What risks does it take? Why are some investments accepted and others rejected? What is the relationship between growth, debt, profit and liquidity?</p>

<p>It is just as important to understand that ownership is not an unlimited right to personal spending.</p>

<p>Capital has its own economic logic. Its value depends on the quality of decisions, the people who manage it and the company's ability to create value over the long term.</p>

<p>A future owner needs to learn to distinguish the interests of the family from the interests of an individual family member, and to understand that those interests will not always fully coincide.</p>

<p>They need to develop the ability to talk with the other owners, to accept different opinions and to make decisions that may not suit everyone equally.</p>

<p>These are capabilities that are hard to develop if the next generation is kept away from important conversations for years and then suddenly handed the ownership.</p>

<p>There is also the other extreme.</p>

<p>Some founders believe the best way to prepare their children is to keep explaining what they should do, while never letting them decide for themselves.</p>

<p>That approach can pass on knowledge, but it does not necessarily develop responsibility.</p>

<p>Responsibility develops when a person is given room to decide, understands the consequences and learns to live with them.</p>

<p>That does not mean future owners should experiment with the family's entire wealth.</p>

<p>It means that development should begin early enough, and in circumstances where mistakes are still bearable.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">It is better for an heir to learn to make decisions while the founder can still help than to decide for real for the first time once the founder is gone.</div>

<h2>The most important inheritance may not be the capital</h2>

<p>When we think about generational transfer, we usually talk about the value of the company, ownership stakes, property and financial assets.</p>

<p>All of that matters.</p>

<p>But the long-term future of family wealth does not depend only on how much capital is transferred.</p>

<p>It also depends on what kind of people have inherited it.</p>

<p>Do they understand its value? Can they tell a right from a responsibility? Can they work with the other owners? Are they capable of making decisions that will not always be comfortable, but may be necessary?</p>

<p>Founders often want to leave their children enough that they never have to go through the hardships they went through themselves.</p>

<p>That wish is understandable.</p>

<p>Yet it was precisely some of those hardships that shaped the capabilities which allowed the founder to create the wealth.</p>

<p>The next generation does not have to repeat the same path. It does not have to go through the same crises or prove its worth in the same way.</p>

<p>But it does have to develop its own capability to carry responsibility.</p>

<p>Otherwise there is a danger that it inherits the results of the parents' work, but not the capability to preserve them.</p>

<p>That is why the question of generational transfer should not only be who will inherit how much.</p>

<p>Just as important is the question of what capability to manage the inherited capital the family will build before the transfer itself.</p>

<p>Because ownership stakes can be divided.</p>

<p>Capital can be transferred.</p>

<p>Rights can be inherited.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">But the capability for responsible ownership is something every generation has to develop for itself.</div>

<p>Continuity does not just happen. It is built.</p>
