<p>In a family business, decisions are made at different levels: family, ownership and management. The family talks about shared values and expectations, the owners decide on matters that belong to their ownership rights, and management runs the business within its remit. When those boundaries are not clear, parallel authorities, informal influence and conflicts emerge that can endanger both the business and family relationships.</p>

<p>Two years ago, a son took over as managing director of the family business.</p>

<p>His father formally stepped back from operational management but remained the majority owner. He is no longer involved in the business day to day, yet he comes to the office regularly and keeps up his relationships with long-serving employees.</p>

<p>One morning, the managing director decides to reorganise the sales department.</p>

<p>He believes the existing way of working no longer suits the size of the company. He wants to change the responsibilities of individual managers and introduce a different system for tracking results.</p>

<p>One long-serving employee is unhappy with the proposed change.</p>

<p>Instead of talking to the managing director, he goes to the founder.</p>

<p>They have known each other for more than twenty years. They went through hard times together and built a relationship that goes beyond ordinary professional collaboration.</p>

<p>The founder hears him out and concludes that the reorganisation should perhaps be postponed.</p>

<p>He calls his son and suggests he think it over once more.</p>

<p>The son explains why the change is needed.</p>

<p>The father replies that he understands, but that he does not want to needlessly unsettle people who spent years building the company.</p>

<p>A few days later, the reorganisation is stopped.</p>

<p>The employees are pleased that the founder intervened. The son is frustrated because he is no longer sure how much he can really decide.</p>

<p>The father, for his part, believes he has simply protected the company from unnecessary conflict.</p>

<p>Nobody necessarily had bad intentions.</p>

<p>But the organisation has received a clear message.</p>

<p>The managing director makes the decisions, except when the founder disagrees with them.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">The problem is not just which decision was better. The problem is that nobody knows any more who is really responsible for it.</div>

<h2>When one person decides everything for years</h2>

<p>In the first generation of a family business, the boundaries between ownership and management often barely exist.</p>

<p>The founder is the owner, the managing director, the chief negotiator and the person who makes the final decisions.</p>

<p>They decide on investments, hiring, financing, the development of new products and relationships with key customers.</p>

<p>They often also decide on matters that would belong to managers in larger organisations.</p>

<p>That way of working can be effective in the early stages of a company's development.</p>

<p>Decisions are made quickly. Responsibility is concentrated. People know who to turn to.</p>

<p>But as the company grows, and the next generation and professional management join the business, the same way of operating starts to create problems.</p>

<p>The founder may no longer be the managing director, but employees still see them as the person with the greatest authority.</p>

<p>The successor formally runs the business, but the founder retains the ability to exert informal influence.</p>

<p>Other family members may hold ownership stakes and expect their opinion to be taken into account, even on day-to-day business decisions.</p>

<p>Managers then find themselves in a situation where it is not enough to know who they formally report to.</p>

<p>They also have to understand who in the family holds real power.</p>

<p>That gradually changes the way the organisation works.</p>

<p>Instead of decisions being made on the basis of clear responsibilities, people start seeking the backing of the person they believe has the greatest influence.</p>

<p>If they disagree with the managing director, they try to talk to the owner.</p>

<p>If the owner is not satisfied, they can bypass management and speak to managers directly.</p>

<p>If a family member wants to change something, they may try to exert influence through private relationships rather than through formal decision-making mechanisms.</p>

<p>A system like that can work as long as the founder has enough energy and authority to reconcile all the interests personally.</p>

<p>But that is exactly where its weakness lies.</p>

<p>The company does not develop the ability to make decisions on its own.</p>

<p>It simply goes on depending on the person who was the centre of every decision for decades.</p>

<h2>Three decision-making spaces families often confuse</h2>

<p>In a family business, it is useful to distinguish three spaces in which different kinds of decisions arise.</p>

<p>The first is the <strong>family</strong>.</p>

<p>The family talks about relationships, shared values, expectations and the long-term vision of the family legacy.</p>

<p>It is the space for talking about whether family members want to stay connected through shared ownership, what role the company plays in their lives and how they want to prepare the next generation.</p>

<p>Such conversations matter because ownership decisions often have deep emotional consequences.</p>

<p>But a family conversation is not, in itself, the same as a formal ownership decision.</p>

<p>The second space is <strong>ownership</strong>.</p>

<p>Owners have certain rights and responsibilities that come with capital.</p>

<p>Within the law and the company's own rules, they decide on matters that belong at the ownership level, such as appointing and overseeing the appropriate bodies, distributing profit, and certain major changes to the company's structure and future.</p>

<p>The ownership role does not mean the right to manage employees day to day or to give instructions directly to managers.</p>

<p>The third space is <strong>management</strong>.</p>

<p>Management is responsible for running the business, organising the work, executing the strategy and delivering results within its remit.</p>

<p>It needs enough room to make the decisions it will answer for.</p>

<p>If the owners expect results but constantly intervene in operational matters at the same time, management's responsibility becomes unclear.</p>

<p>The three levels are interconnected.</p>

<p>Family values can shape long-term ownership expectations. The owners set a framework within their authority, and management runs the company in line with that framework.</p>

<p>But being connected does not mean the roles can be swapped at will.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">The family can have a shared vision. The owners can have shared capital. Management must have the clear right and responsibility to run the business.</div>

<h2>Being an owner does not mean deciding everything</h2>

<p>One common misconception in family businesses is the belief that an ownership stake automatically grants the right to decide on every business matter.</p>

<p>For a founder, that way of thinking is understandable.</p>

<p>For decades they were both owner and managing director. Almost every important decision passed through them.</p>

<p>When they step back from management, it can be hard to accept that they should no longer decide on the day-to-day running of the business.</p>

<p>Especially when they believe they would have made a particular decision better than the new managing director.</p>

<p>But ownership and management carry different responsibilities.</p>

<p>An owner has a legitimate right to protect their assets, to take part in the decisions that belong to them and to expect capital to be managed responsibly.</p>

<p>But if, at the same time, they want to decide on every employee, supplier or operational process, management loses the ability to genuinely lead.</p>

<p>A serious imbalance then appears.</p>

<p>The managing director answers for the results but does not have full control over the decisions that lead to them.</p>

<p>The owner influences decisions but may not take day-to-day responsibility for carrying them out.</p>

<p>When the result is poor, it becomes hard to establish who is actually responsible.</p>

<p>The managing director can claim they had no freedom to decide.</p>

<p>The owner can conclude that the managing director is not capable enough.</p>

<p>And the organisation is left caught between them.</p>

<p>That is why a clear delineation of authority is not an attempt to restrict the owner.</p>

<p>It is a way of protecting the quality of management and enabling real accountability for results.</p>

<p>That does not mean the owner has to be passive.</p>

<p>They can set clear expectations, require reporting, take part in strategic discussions and use their rights when management does not deliver the expected results.</p>

<p>But there is a difference between overseeing management and taking over management decisions day by day.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">We cannot hold someone responsible for results if, at the same time, we do not let them make the decisions needed to achieve them.</div>

<h2>Family relationships should not replace business authority</h2>

<p>The particular complexity of a family business comes from the fact that business relationships are constantly interwoven with personal ones.</p>

<p>The father may be the majority owner, the son the managing director, the daughter a member of the supervisory board, and the brother a co-owner who does not work in the company.</p>

<p>In business terms, each of them has a defined role.</p>

<p>But in family terms they are still father, son, daughter and brother.</p>

<p>Those personal roles carry their own history, emotions and patterns of behaviour.</p>

<p>A son who is managing director may find it hard to stand up to his father, because he has been used to respecting his father's authority all his life.</p>

<p>The father may struggle to accept that his son now has the right to make a decision he personally disagrees with.</p>

<p>A brother who is not employed in the company may expect his opinion to be heeded because he is a family member, even though he has no formal management role.</p>

<p>Situations like these are not just an organisational problem.</p>

<p>They are also a question of changing relationships between family members.</p>

<p>While the child was small, the parent naturally made the decisions.</p>

<p>While the successor was learning the business, the founder had more knowledge and experience.</p>

<p>But when the successor takes over responsibility for leading the company, the relationship has to change.</p>

<p>Father and son can keep the same family relationship, but their business roles are no longer the same.</p>

<p>That calls for the ability to distinguish a personal opinion from formal authority.</p>

<p>The founder may see a particular decision differently.</p>

<p>They can voice that view, argue for it and discuss it.</p>

<p>But if the decision belongs to management, and there is no reason to intervene through the appropriate formal procedure, their personal preference should not automatically become the final decision.</p>

<p>Equally, the successor should not expect their role as managing director to strip the owner of every right to oversee and to ask questions.</p>

<p>Maturity in family governance does not mean family members stop having different opinions.</p>

<p>It means they know the difference between the right to an opinion and the right to a decision.</p>

<h2>The most dangerous decisions are the ones nobody is clearly responsible for</h2>

<p>Not all decisions in a family business are equally important.</p>

<p>Some concern day-to-day business.</p>

<p>Others can change the future of the whole company or of the family's capital.</p>

<p>Hiring a sales manager and selling a majority stake in the company, for instance, are not decisions of the same nature.</p>

<p>A decision to change supplier and a decision to enter an entirely new industry may call for different levels of involvement from the owners and from management.</p>

<p>The same goes for large investments, significant borrowing or changes to the ownership structure.</p>

<p>That is exactly why it is not enough to say that the managing director runs the company and the owners decide on ownership.</p>

<p>What matters is understanding where the boundaries lie.</p>

<p>Which decisions can management make on its own?</p>

<p>Which decisions need the prior approval of the appropriate body?</p>

<p>Which decisions belong to the owners?</p>

<p>And which matters should the family discuss in advance so that expectations are understood, even though the family itself is not a formal decision-making body?</p>

<p>The answers depend on the size of the company, the ownership structure, the legal form and the agreed system of governance.</p>

<p>But one principle stays the same.</p>

<p>For every important decision it should be clear who has the authority to decide and who is responsible for the consequences.</p>

<p>When that is not clear, decisions can be postponed, passed back and forth between people or made through informal channels.</p>

<p>Managers then do not know whose instructions to follow.</p>

<p>Owners can feel they do not have enough influence.</p>

<p>Managing directors can feel they carry responsibility without real authority.</p>

<p>And the family can start to experience every business misunderstanding as a personal conflict.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">Unclear authority does not just produce bad decisions. It produces a system in which nobody can fully take responsibility.</div>

<h2>The leadership handover is not finished until the right to decide is handed over too</h2>

<p>A founder can appoint the successor as managing director.</p>

<p>They can introduce the successor to employees, customers and business partners.</p>

<p>They can formally withdraw from management.</p>

<p>But if every important decision still ends up with them, the real handover of leadership is not complete.</p>

<p>That does not have to mean the founder should distance themselves from the company entirely.</p>

<p>They can remain an owner, a member of the appropriate supervisory body or an adviser to the next generation.</p>

<p>Their experience and relationships can be exceptionally valuable.</p>

<p>The new role, however, demands a different way of acting.</p>

<p>Instead of solving every problem single-handedly, the founder can help the successor think better.</p>

<p>Instead of giving instructions directly to managers, they can raise their concerns through the agreed channels.</p>

<p>Instead of informally overturning management's decisions, they can use the mechanisms of oversight and ownership decision-making that fit their role.</p>

<p>That change is often more demanding than the formal transfer of the position itself.</p>

<p>Because the founder is not just handing over certain powers.</p>

<p>They have to accept that the company will no longer always run according to their personal preferences.</p>

<p>And the successor has to accept that greater independence brings greater responsibility.</p>

<p>They will no longer be able to leave every hard decision to the founder.</p>

<p>They will not be able to expect someone else to bear the consequences of their mistakes.</p>

<p>And they will not be able to build authority on the family name alone.</p>

<p>A successful transition requires both changes.</p>

<p>The founder has to learn to let go of the decisions that no longer belong to their role.</p>

<p>The successor has to learn to carry responsibility for the decisions that now belong to them.</p>

<h2>Clear boundaries can protect family relationships</h2>

<p>Families sometimes avoid formalising the way decisions are made because they believe it could damage the trust between them.</p>

<p>If we are family, why would we need to define who decides what?</p>

<p>Why would we introduce formal rules when we can talk and come to an agreement?</p>

<p>That approach can work while relationships are simple, the owners few and expectations aligned.</p>

<p>But as the company grows and ownership passes to more family members, informal arrangements become ever more fragile.</p>

<p>What was obvious to one person may never have been said out loud to another.</p>

<p>What the founder saw as a temporary arrangement, the successor may experience as a permanent right.</p>

<p>What one family member considers advice, another may experience as pressure.</p>

<p>Clear rules, then, are not necessarily a sign of distrust.</p>

<p>They can be a way of protecting trust.</p>

<p>When people understand their roles, it is easier for them to accept disagreeing on a particular issue.</p>

<p>They can talk, make their case and try to influence the decision.</p>

<p>But they know who, in the end, has the authority to decide.</p>

<p>That does not remove every conflict.</p>

<p>But it can stop every disagreement turning into a battle for authority.</p>

<p>That is precisely one of the important steps in the development of a family business.</p>

<p>The move from a system that depends on the founder's personal authority to a system in which responsibilities and authority can work independently of any one person.</p>

<h2>Who decides what?</h2>

<p>On the face of it, it is a simple question.</p>

<p>But the answer reveals how ready a family business really is for the future.</p>

<p>If every important decision still needs the founder's approval, the company may have changed its managing director, but it has not changed the way it is governed.</p>

<p>If every family member believes they have the right to intervene in the business, ownership has not been clearly separated from management.</p>

<p>If the managing director answers for results but cannot make decisions independently within their remit, their accountability is not real.</p>

<p>And if the owners have no way of understanding and overseeing how the capital is managed, then the ownership role is not sufficiently developed either.</p>

<p>That is why the question of who decides what is not merely a technical question of organisation.</p>

<p>It is a question of the maturity of the family business.</p>

<p>It shows whether the family can tell personal relationships apart from business responsibilities.</p>

<p>Whether the founder can accept a different role.</p>

<p>Whether the next generation can develop authority of its own.</p>

<p>And whether the company can go on making good decisions without constantly relying on one person.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">Continuity does not require everyone to decide everything. It requires everyone to understand their own responsibility and respect the boundaries of other people's.</div>

<p>Continuity does not just happen. It is built.</p>
