<p>Dividing ownership equally among the children is often seen as the fairest outcome of a generational transition. Yet when the heirs have different roles, responsibilities and contributions, equal ownership stakes do not necessarily mean a fair arrangement. Fairness in a family business is not determined only by how much each person received, but also by how well ownership, responsibility, contribution and the family members' expectations are aligned.</p>

<p>A father has spent thirty-five years building a family business. Today it employs more than a hundred people and makes up the largest part of the family's wealth.</p>

<p>He has three children.</p>

<p>The eldest son has worked in the company for almost twenty years. He started in operational roles, gradually took on responsibility, and today runs the largest part of the business. The father is stepping back more and more, and the son has become the key person for employees, customers and suppliers.</p>

<p>The second child moved abroad and built a career of their own. The third lives in Croatia but has never shown any interest in the family business.</p>

<p>The father is proud of all his children.</p>

<p>When he starts thinking about transferring ownership, his decision is simple.</p>

<p>"We will divide everything into three equal parts. I do not want my children fighting one day."</p>

<p>For him, this is a question of love and fairness. He does not want to hurt anyone, or leave the impression that one child is worth more than another.</p>

<p>But the son who runs the company sees that decision differently.</p>

<p>For years he has taken on responsibility, given up other professional opportunities and built the business alongside his father. Now he wonders why he will be deciding on the company's future together with a brother and sister who have never worked in it.</p>

<p>His brother and sister see it from a different perspective. The company was created by their father. They are all his children, and they believe they have an equal right to the family inheritance.</p>

<p>No one is necessarily being selfish. No one has to have bad intentions.</p>

<p>And yet all of them can feel they have not been treated fairly.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">An equal division can settle the question of how much each person received while leaving open the question of what each person took on.</div>

<h2>Why parents equate equality with fairness</h2>

<p>Parents naturally want to love their children equally.</p>

<p>When the time comes to divide the family's wealth, they often try to carry that emotional equality over into the ownership arrangements.</p>

<p>If every child receives an equal share, no one should be hurt. No one should think they are worth less or loved less.</p>

<p>That logic is understandable.</p>

<p>But a family and a business do not work by exactly the same rules.</p>

<p>In a family, all children have equal worth as people. Their belonging to the family does not depend on professional success, abilities or contribution to the business.</p>

<p>In a business, however, different roles carry different responsibilities, risks and consequences.</p>

<p>The child who runs the company makes daily decisions about employees, investments, customers and business development. The child who is not involved in the business can have entirely legitimate ownership interests, but does not carry the same operational responsibility.</p>

<p>That does not mean one child should automatically receive more ownership.</p>

<p>It only means that equal belonging to the family does not answer every question about the future ownership structure.</p>

<p>The problem arises when parents try to resolve the emotional question of equality among their children through the division of ownership.</p>

<p>Ownership stakes then become a symbol of love.</p>

<p>And any discussion of a different division can be experienced as a message that one child is worth more than another.</p>

<p>In those circumstances it becomes hard for the family to talk about the real differences between responsibilities, contributions and future expectations.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">The equal worth of children as members of the family does not mean their roles in the family business have to be equal.</div>

<h2>Contribution, responsibility and ownership are not the same thing</h2>

<p>One of the most common tensions arises between children who actively work in the family business and those who have chosen a different professional path.</p>

<p>The active family member can feel they have earned a larger ownership stake because they spent years developing the business.</p>

<p>The other members can feel that this calls into question their right to the family inheritance.</p>

<p>But before the family tries to decide who deserves how much, it is important to distinguish three things.</p>

<p>The first is ownership, which stems from the wealth the founder created and decided to pass on.</p>

<p>The second is the professional contribution of the person who works in the company. That contribution can be recognised through salary, bonuses, long-term incentives or other forms of reward.</p>

<p>The third is responsibility for the future management and development of the company.</p>

<p>These three things are connected, but they are not identical.</p>

<p>A child who has worked in the company for twenty years may have been properly paid for their work. They may already have had a share in the additional value created, through special rewards or ownership incentives.</p>

<p>But they may also have worked for years below market terms, taken personal risks and given up other opportunities because they believed they were building something they would one day lead and own.</p>

<p>Those two situations are not the same.</p>

<p>Equally, a child who has not worked in the company may have built their own career without using family capital. Their decisions may be exactly what allowed family relationships to stay simpler and the company to be run by the person who genuinely wants to do it.</p>

<p>That is why fairness cannot be established simply by comparing the number of years spent in the company.</p>

<p>Nor can it be concluded that whoever runs the business should automatically own a larger share of the capital.</p>

<p>What matters is understanding what has already been rewarded, what expectations have been created, and what responsibilities individual family members will take on in the future.</p>

<p>Without that conversation, the division of ownership easily becomes an attempt to settle up every family contribution after the fact.</p>

<p>And that kind of reckoning can almost never be completely objective.</p>

<h2>Equal stakes do not mean equal power or equal interests</h2>

<p>Imagine three children each inheriting a third of the ownership.</p>

<p>On paper, they are all equal.</p>

<p>But one child runs the company and earns their professional income from it. Another has their own career and expects a long-term return from the family ownership. The third may want regular dividends to finance their everyday needs.</p>

<p>Their interests can differ even though their ownership stakes are equal.</p>

<p>The managing director may argue for reinvesting profits, developing new products or making acquisitions.</p>

<p>The other owners may prefer greater financial security, lower debt or a regular distribution of profits.</p>

<p>Such differences are not necessarily a sign of conflict.</p>

<p>They are a natural consequence of different life circumstances and different attitudes to capital.</p>

<p>The problem arises when the family assumes that equal ownership stakes will, by themselves, guarantee unity.</p>

<p>They will not.</p>

<p>Ownership defines rights, but it does not automatically create a shared vision.</p>

<p>If the family has not talked about what it wants from the company, how it will make important decisions and how it will reconcile different interests, an equal division may only postpone future disagreements.</p>

<p>A particularly delicate situation is one in which the person running the company cannot carry important decisions through on their own, while the other owners do not understand the business well enough to decide on them properly.</p>

<p>On the other hand, it is no better when the managing director believes their operational role gives them the right to ignore the interests of the other owners.</p>

<p>A successful ownership structure therefore does not depend on percentages alone.</p>

<p>It depends on the family's ability to distinguish ownership rights, management authority and personal interests.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">Equal ownership stakes do not automatically create equal interests. And different interests need not destroy family unity if there are clear rules for reconciling them.</div>

<h2>An unequal division can be unfair too</h2>

<p>It would be wrong to conclude from all this that the child who works in the family business should receive a larger ownership stake.</p>

<p>Such a decision can be justified in certain circumstances, but there is no universal rule.</p>

<p>Sometimes a founder gives a larger stake to the child who works in the company, believing that child will best preserve the business.</p>

<p>But in doing so they can unintentionally create a feeling that the other children are worth less, or that their professional choices are being punished.</p>

<p>A child who has built a career outside the family business is not necessarily less capable, less responsible or less interested in the family's capital.</p>

<p>Perhaps they simply did not want to be the managing director.</p>

<p>If ownership is divided solely according to who stayed to work in the company, the family may send the message that belonging and loyalty are measured by professional choice.</p>

<p>That can have long-term consequences for the relationships between siblings.</p>

<p>Equally, the child who runs the company should not assume that a larger ownership stake is automatically guaranteed simply because they have taken on the management role.</p>

<p>Professional responsibility must have clear criteria, and the division of ownership should follow from a broader understanding of the family's wealth, contributions, expectations and future.</p>

<p>Fairness therefore does not mean everyone has to receive the same.</p>

<p>But nor does it mean that those who work in the company have to receive more.</p>

<p>It requires the family to understand the reasons behind a particular decision and the consequences that decision will have for everyone involved.</p>

<p>Sometimes an equal division will be the best solution.</p>

<p>Sometimes a different division will suit the real circumstances better.</p>

<p>And sometimes fairness can be achieved through a combination of different kinds of assets, professional reward and clearly defined ownership rights.</p>

<p>What matters is that the decision does not come only from a desire to avoid an uncomfortable conversation.</p>

<h2>Fairness cannot be left for the day of inheritance</h2>

<p>Founders often believe that one day the children will find a way to agree among themselves.</p>

<p>Perhaps they will.</p>

<p>But if they have not spent years talking about their expectations, contributions and future roles, it is hard to expect such an agreement simply to appear once ownership has been transferred.</p>

<p>Especially once the founder is no longer there as the person who held the family together for decades and made the final decisions.</p>

<p>At that point, the question of ownership stakes can turn into a much deeper debate.</p>

<p>Who was closer to the parents? Who sacrificed more? Who had more opportunities? Who received more support? Who spent their whole life in the shadow of the family business?</p>

<p>Conversations like these are rarely only about capital.</p>

<p>They are about recognition, belonging and a person's sense of their own worth within the family.</p>

<p>That is why the conversation about fairness should begin while the founder can still explain their intentions, hear different perspectives and help the family understand the consequences of particular decisions.</p>

<p>That does not mean every family member has to be happy with every solution.</p>

<p>Nor does it mean that fairness requires unanimity.</p>

<p>But there is a great difference between a decision someone dislikes but understands, and a decision they experience as an inexplicable injustice.</p>

<p>The family may not manage to remove every difference in expectations.</p>

<p>It can, however, prevent those differences from becoming visible for the first time only when there is no longer enough time to talk.</p>

<h2>Fairness is not a mathematical formula</h2>

<p>When a founder thinks about the future of the family business, it is natural that they want to find a solution that will be fair to all their children.</p>

<p>But fairness cannot always be expressed as a percentage of ownership.</p>

<p>Two children can receive the same and feel completely differently about it.</p>

<p>One may receive greater responsibility, the other greater freedom. One may have a daily influence on the business, the other only an economic interest. One may be tied to the company professionally and emotionally, the other may see it as part of the family's wealth.</p>

<p>None of this, by itself, determines what is fair.</p>

<p>But these are all circumstances that need to be understood before the decision is made.</p>

<p>Perhaps the greatest mistake is to believe that fairness in a family can be secured by simply dividing the capital into equal parts.</p>

<p>Because what is being divided is not only wealth.</p>

<p>Future rights, responsibilities, opportunities and expectations are being divided too.</p>

<p>And those are never completely equal.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">Fairness does not mean everyone has to receive the same. It means that the differences between people, their roles and their responsibilities must not be ignored when we decide on a shared future.</div>

<p>Continuity does not just happen. It is built.</p>
