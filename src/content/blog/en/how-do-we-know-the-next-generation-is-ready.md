<p>The next generation's readiness to take over the family business is not determined only by education, experience or years spent in the business. It is recognised in the ability to make decisions independently, lead people, take responsibility for results and build trust. A successor does not have to know everything the founder knows, but they must show they can lead the company responsibly even when the founder is no longer present.</p>

<p>A father has been thinking about stepping back from operational management for several years.</p>

<p>His son has worked in the company for almost ten years. He has a university degree, knows the business and takes part in important meetings. The employees know him, and the father increasingly introduces him as the future managing director.</p>

<p>Asked whether his son is ready, the father replies that he is not yet entirely sure.</p>

<p>"He is good, he is smart and he works hard. But he still lacks experience."</p>

<p>When he is asked what exactly is missing, the answer becomes less clear.</p>

<p>Perhaps the son should work alongside him for a few more years. Perhaps he should get to know the finances better. Perhaps he is not yet decisive enough. Perhaps he should build more authority among the employees.</p>

<p>The son, on the other hand, believes he is ready.</p>

<p>He has worked in the company for years, understands the business and wants to take on more responsibility. Yet almost every important decision still ends up on his father's desk.</p>

<p>The father is waiting for the son to become ready.</p>

<p>The son is waiting for the father to let him show that he is ready.</p>

<p>And the company carries on working just as it has for the past ten years.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">Sometimes the biggest problem is not that the successor is not ready, but that the family has never defined what readiness actually means.</div>

<h2>Readiness is not the same as experience</h2>

<p>In family businesses, a successor's readiness is often judged by their education, years of work and knowledge of the business.</p>

<p>These are important elements of professional development, but on their own they are not enough.</p>

<p>A person can work in a company for ten years without ever having independently led people, made important decisions or answered for a financial result.</p>

<p>They can know all the key customers, products and processes without ever having had to decide which business relationship to end, which investment to turn down or which manager to replace.</p>

<p>They can be exceptionally well educated, yet struggle in situations where there are no clear answers.</p>

<p>Leadership does not require knowledge alone.</p>

<p>It requires the ability to exercise judgement under uncertainty.</p>

<p>Founders often developed that ability over decades in business. They learnt through decisions that had real consequences for their capital, their employees and their family.</p>

<p>The next generation does not have to repeat the same journey.</p>

<p>But it does have to develop its own ability to make decisions.</p>

<p>That is why what matters most is not how long a successor has worked in the company, but what responsibility they have actually carried during that time.</p>

<p>There is a great difference between ten years of experience and one year of experience repeated ten times.</p>

<p>A successor who has spent years carrying out tasks according to their father's instructions may know the business well, but that does not mean they have developed the ability to lead independently.</p>

<p>On the other hand, a person with fewer years of experience can show a high level of maturity if they have already led teams, managed results and borne the consequences of their own decisions.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">Readiness is not measured by time spent in the company, but by the responsibility a person has learnt to carry.</div>

<h2>Five abilities that reveal real readiness</h2>

<p>When we assess whether a successor is ready to take over the leadership, it is more useful to look at their behaviour than their CV.</p>

<p>The first is the ability to <strong>decide independently</strong>.</p>

<p>A successor needs to show they can analyse a situation, consider the options and make a decision without the founder always having to confirm their choice.</p>

<p>That does not mean they should never seek advice.</p>

<p>Good leaders know when to involve other people. The difference is whether the successor seeks advice in order to decide better, or seeks approval because they are afraid to take responsibility.</p>

<p>The second is the ability to <strong>accept the consequences</strong>.</p>

<p>Every important decision carries some risk.</p>

<p>A successor who is ready for leadership does not have to get every decision right, but they must be able to admit a mistake, understand its consequences and take the necessary steps.</p>

<p>If they find an excuse for every failure in the circumstances, the employees or the founder, it is hard to expect them to carry ultimate responsibility for the company successfully.</p>

<p>The third is the ability to <strong>lead people</strong>.</p>

<p>The role of managing director is not only about running the business. It includes developing managers, making uncomfortable decisions, resolving conflicts and building an organisation that can deliver results.</p>

<p>A successor can be an excellent specialist, but that does not automatically make them a good leader.</p>

<p>It is important to watch whether people trust them because they are the founder's child, or because working together has built trust in their ability and character.</p>

<p>The fourth is the ability to <strong>understand the business as a whole</strong>.</p>

<p>A successor does not have to be the best expert in finance, sales, production or technology.</p>

<p>But they must understand how the different parts of the business connect, what creates value and which decisions could endanger the company's long-term viability.</p>

<p>They must know how to ask the right questions and judge the quality of the answers they get from their managers.</p>

<p>The fifth is the ability to <strong>maintain an independent relationship with the founder</strong>.</p>

<p>It is perhaps the most delicate of all.</p>

<p>A successor needs to be able to respect the founder's experience while developing judgement of their own.</p>

<p>They need to be able to voice a different opinion, argue for a decision and accept responsibility for the direction they propose.</p>

<p>If a successor can only function when the founder agrees with them, their authority still depends on the founder's approval.</p>

<p>That does not mean rejecting everything the previous generation built.</p>

<p>It means they must develop the ability to lead independently within the values and long-term interests of the company.</p>

<p>These abilities are not traits that can be reliably assessed in conversation alone.</p>

<p>They become visible through concrete situations, decisions and results.</p>

<h2>The successor does not have to be a copy of the founder</h2>

<p>One of the greatest obstacles to an objective assessment of readiness is the expectation that the successor must resemble the founder.</p>

<p>The founder may have a strong entrepreneurial instinct, make decisions quickly and rely on personal relationships.</p>

<p>The successor may be more analytical, more cautious and more inclined towards structured management.</p>

<p>The founder may feel the successor lacks decisiveness.</p>

<p>The successor may believe the founder makes too many decisions on intuition.</p>

<p>Both can be right in particular situations.</p>

<p>But a different leadership style does not automatically mean a different ability to lead.</p>

<p>A company the founder spent thirty years building may today need a different way of being run than it did in its early days.</p>

<p>The organisation is larger, the market more complex, and responsibility is shared among more people.</p>

<p>The ability the founder needed to create the company is not necessarily identical to the ability needed to develop it further.</p>

<p>That is why the assessment of readiness should not rest on whether the successor thinks like the founder.</p>

<p>What matters more is whether they can make sound decisions, develop people and preserve the company's long-term ability to create value.</p>

<p>If the founder sets their own leadership style as the only measure, there is a danger the successor will never be good enough.</p>

<p>Something will always be missing.</p>

<p>They may not have the same intuition, the same relationship with customers or the same skill in negotiation.</p>

<p>But they may have abilities the founder never developed.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">The goal of a generational transition is not to create a second founder. The goal is to develop a leader who can lead the company in the future.</div>

<h2>It must be possible to prove readiness before leadership is handed over</h2>

<p>How, then, can the family know whether the successor is ready?</p>

<p>Not by endlessly debating their potential.</p>

<p>But by letting them take on responsibility in circumstances where ability can actually be shown.</p>

<p>A successor can be given responsibility for a particular business segment, an investment project or an organisational unit.</p>

<p>What matters is that they have clearly defined goals, appropriate authority and responsibility for the results.</p>

<p>The founder can follow their development, ask questions and offer support, but should not keep taking the decisions over.</p>

<p>Through that kind of experience it becomes visible how the successor thinks, how they react under pressure, how they work with people and how they cope with failure.</p>

<p>It is especially important to watch what happens when things do not go to plan.</p>

<p>Almost anyone can look capable while the business is growing, relationships are stable and decisions carry no serious consequences.</p>

<p>Real maturity becomes visible when an unpopular decision has to be made, a mistake admitted or responsibility taken for a result that was not achieved.</p>

<p>Such situations do not need to be created artificially.</p>

<p>But nor should the successor constantly be protected from them.</p>

<p>If the founder intervenes every time a problem appears, they take away the successor's chance to show how they handle responsibility.</p>

<p>At the same time, they deprive themselves of the chance to assess the successor's readiness objectively.</p>

<p>Because readiness cannot be proven without the opportunity to actually take on responsibility.</p>

<p>That does not mean every mistake has to be allowed.</p>

<p>The limits of risk need to be clear, especially where decisions could endanger a significant part of the family's capital or the future of the company.</p>

<p>But within those limits the successor must have room to act independently.</p>

<p>Otherwise the family is not assessing their ability to lead.</p>

<p>It is assessing their ability to function under the founder's constant supervision.</p>

<h2>And what if the successor is not ready?</h2>

<p>That is a question many families try to avoid.</p>

<p>The founder may have been saying for years that the child will take over the company. The successor has built their own expectations on that assumption, and the employees have come to see them as the future managing director.</p>

<p>Admitting that a person is not yet ready can be emotionally difficult.</p>

<p>The founder can feel they have failed to prepare their child.</p>

<p>The successor can conclude that their father does not trust them.</p>

<p>And the family can begin to experience an assessment of professional ability as an assessment of personal worth.</p>

<p>But those two things are not the same.</p>

<p>A person can be responsible, educated and successful in many areas and still not yet be ready to lead a complex family business.</p>

<p>Perhaps they need more experience.</p>

<p>Perhaps they need to develop certain abilities.</p>

<p>And perhaps running a company is simply not the role that best suits their interests and potential.</p>

<p>That does not have to mean the generational transition has failed.</p>

<p>Sometimes the family's very ability to recognise such a situation is a sign of its maturity.</p>

<p>The successor can continue their professional development. They can take on a different role. They can be a responsible owner while professional management runs the day-to-day business.</p>

<p>The greatest risk is not necessarily that the successor is not ready.</p>

<p>A greater risk can arise when the family does not want to admit it, and the role is handed over simply because for years it was expected to happen.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">A successor does not have to become the managing director to be a valued member of the family. And the company should not become the place where their worth must be proven at any cost.</div>

<h2>Readiness is the responsibility of both generations</h2>

<p>When a founder says the successor is not yet ready, a second question is worth asking.</p>

<p>What has the family done so far to allow them to become ready?</p>

<p>Have they had the opportunity to make decisions independently?</p>

<p>Have they led people and answered for results?</p>

<p>Have they been able to develop their own authority, or did every important decision still come back to the founder?</p>

<p>Has the family clearly defined what it expects of the future leader?</p>

<p>If the answers to those questions are not clear, the problem may not lie only with the successor.</p>

<p>It may also lie in the way their preparation was carried out.</p>

<p>A founder cannot hand over their own experience, but they can create the circumstances in which the next generation will develop its own.</p>

<p>They can gradually hand over responsibility, open up room for independent decision-making and allow the successor to develop a different style of leadership.</p>

<p>The successor, on the other hand, cannot expect readiness to be acknowledged simply because they want the role.</p>

<p>They must show initiative, develop their abilities and take responsibility for results.</p>

<p>Preparing for leadership is therefore not a one-sided obligation.</p>

<p>It requires the successor to develop and the founder to change their behaviour.</p>

<p>Only when both changes happen can the company gradually stop depending on one person.</p>

<h2>The real question is not whether the successor is ready for everything</h2>

<p>No leader is completely ready for every situation that awaits them.</p>

<p>The founder did not know everything when they started the business either.</p>

<p>Over the years they learnt, made mistakes, developed abilities and adapted to circumstances.</p>

<p>The next generation will have to do the same.</p>

<p>That is why the question of readiness should not be an attempt to find a person who will never make a mistake.</p>

<p>It is more important to understand whether the successor has developed enough ability, maturity and independence to take responsibility for the company's further development.</p>

<p>Can they make decisions without constantly leaning on the founder?</p>

<p>Can they lead people who may have worked with their parents for decades?</p>

<p>Can they accept responsibility when the result is not good?</p>

<p>Can they develop their own authority without constantly having to prove they are the founder's equal?</p>

<p>And perhaps most importantly, can the company keep working once the founder stops being the person who solves every serious problem?</p>

<p>If the family can answer those questions on the basis of real experience rather than expectation alone, it has a much clearer picture of the next generation's readiness.</p>

<p>Readiness is not the moment when every doubt disappears.</p>

<p>It is the moment when there are strong enough reasons to trust that the new generation can carry responsibility on its own.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">A successor is not ready when the founder has nothing left to decide. They are ready when the founder no longer has to decide for them.</div>

<p>Continuity does not just happen. It is built.</p>
