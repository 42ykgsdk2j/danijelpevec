<p>Most families picture a generational transition as a handover.</p>

<p>Someone leaves. Someone else takes over. Documents are signed. Titles change. Ownership is transferred.</p>

<p>On paper, a transition can look complete in a single day. In reality, that is often the day it begins.</p>

<p>A generational transition is not an event in which one person stops being responsible and another starts. It is a change to the whole system that plays out over years.</p>

<p>Far more changes than the name attached to a title.</p>

<h2>1. The founder's role changes</h2>

<p>This may be the hardest change of all.</p>

<p>For decades, the founder has often been the person who:</p>

<ul>
<li>made the final decisions</li>
<li>carried the greatest risk</li>
<li>knew the people and the business better than anyone</li>
<li>solved the problems no one else could solve</li>
<li>was the final authority.</li>
</ul>

<p>The problem arises when the formal position changes but the real role does not.</p>

<p>The founder is no longer the CEO, but still makes the key decisions. No longer formally responsible, but everyone still waits for their approval. They say they have handed the business to the next generation, but the system still behaves as though it has to ask them.</p>

<p>That is not a transition. That is a change of title.</p>

<p>A real transition begins when the founder gradually changes their function in the system. From the person who leads to the person who enables. From making decisions to developing other people's ability to make them.</p>

<p>That is far harder than handing over a title.</p>

<h2>2. Authority changes</h2>

<p>Every family company has formal authority. It also has real authority.</p>

<p>The two are not always the same.</p>

<p>There can be a new CEO, a new board chair or a new owner, and people will still look to the founder when an important decision has to be made.</p>

<p>So the question of transition is not only:</p>

<p><strong>Who holds the position?</strong></p>

<p>More important is:</p>

<p><strong>Who has the right to decide, and who trusts them to make that decision?</strong></p>

<p>Authority is not transferred by signature. It has to be built gradually. And this is exactly where many transitions become uncomfortable.</p>

<p>The next generation may formally hold the authority, but not yet enough experience.</p>

<p>The founder may want to let go, but does not yet have enough trust.</p>

<p>Professional management may support the new generation, but is not sure who will really have the last word.</p>

<p>The formal structure can look perfect.</p>

<p>The real structure of power can be completely different.</p>

<h2>3. The next generation's responsibility changes</h2>

<p>A successor does not just take over a position. They take over the consequences of decisions. That is a big difference.</p>

<p>Being a member of a family that owns a business is not the same as being a responsible owner. Being the founder's child is not the same as being a person other people need to trust.</p>

<p>That is why the next generation has to develop its own capacity for making decisions. It does not need to become a copy of the founder. In fact, it should not.</p>

<p>Every generation has to find its own way of creating value, leading people and carrying responsibility.</p>

<p>The goal of a transition is not to reproduce the person who built the business. The goal is to develop a generation that can run it responsibly without that person.</p>

<h2>4. Ownership changes</h2>

<p>In a transition, too much attention often goes to one question:</p>

<p><strong>Who gets which shares?</strong></p>

<p>It is an important question. But it is not the only one.</p>

<p>Ownership brings rights, but also responsibilities.</p>

<p>What does it mean to be an owner if you are no longer involved in operations?</p>

<p>How do you decide on dividends?</p>

<p>How much do you reinvest?</p>

<p>What if one family member wants to sell and another wants to hold?</p>

<p>What does fairness mean between active and non-active owners?</p>

<p>Can an owner understand the business well enough to decide responsibly about its future?</p>

<p>That is why the generational transfer of ownership is not only a legal or financial event. It is a transfer of responsibility.</p>

<p>Ownership has to become something the next generation is capable of carrying.</p>

<h2>5. Family relationships change</h2>

<p>This is the part of a transition that is most often underestimated.</p>

<p>While the founder is the dominant figure, many family differences can stay hidden.</p>

<p>Everyone knows who decides. When that person starts to release control, questions that sat below the surface for years come up:</p>

<ul>
<li>Who contributed the most?</li>
<li>Who has the right to decide?</li>
<li>Was everyone offered the same opportunity?</li>
<li>What about the family member who does not want to work in the business?</li>
<li>What do spouses bring into the system?</li>
<li>What if one branch of the family wants growth and another wants liquidity?</li>
</ul>

<p>A transition, then, often does not create problems. It makes them visible.</p>

<p>That is why one of the most dangerous assumptions is that "the family will sort it out somehow".</p>

<p>If the important things are not discussed before they become urgent, the system will have to deal with them when the stakes are already much higher.</p>

<div class="pull-quote">A transition often does not create problems. It makes them visible.</div>

<h2>6. The relationship with the business changes</h2>

<p>A business built by its founder often carries the founder's personal identity.</p>

<p>It is not just a company. It is part of their life story.</p>

<p>That is why it is hard for a founder to tell apart:</p>

<p><strong>"this is my company"</strong></p>

<p>from</p>

<p><strong>"this is what I built."</strong></p>

<p>The next generation enters a business already shaped by someone else's decisions. Their task is not only to preserve what exists.</p>

<p>They have to decide what should stay, what should change and what no longer belongs to the future.</p>

<p>That takes enough respect for the past not to discard it, and enough freedom not to have to repeat it.</p>

<h2>7. The relationship with capital changes</h2>

<p>The first generation often thinks like an entrepreneur. Capital is used for growth. Risk is personal. Decisions are fast.</p>

<p>The next generation can find itself in a completely different situation. It is no longer managing only money it created itself. It is managing capital it received and is expected to pass on.</p>

<p>Then the question changes. It is no longer only:</p>

<p><strong>How can we grow the wealth?</strong></p>

<p>But:</p>

<p><strong>How will we manage it responsibly through the next decade and the next generation?</strong></p>

<p>This is the shift from "creating" to "preserving". And it is often one of the most important, yet least visible, parts of a generational transition.</p>

<h2>8. The family's identity changes</h2>

<p>In the end, something even deeper changes.</p>

<p>The question: <strong>"Who are we as a family?"</strong></p>

<p>While the founder runs the business, the family's identity and the business's identity are often intertwined.</p>

<p>After the transition it is no longer enough to ask: <strong>What did our father build?</strong></p>

<p>We need to ask:</p>

<p><strong>What do we want to preserve?</strong></p>

<p><strong>What do we want to change?</strong></p>

<p><strong>Why do we want to remain owners at all?</strong></p>

<p><strong>What responsibility do we want to have towards the people who work in the company?</strong></p>

<p><strong>What do we want to pass on to our children besides wealth?</strong></p>

<p>These are questions of continuity.</p>

<p>Because continuity does not mean everything stays the same. Continuity means that what is valuable can survive change.</p>

<h2>Why a transition is not the transfer of one thing</h2>

<p>If we look at a generational transition only as succession planning, we can miss what matters most.</p>

<p>During a transition, all of these change:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>roles</strong></li>
<li><strong>authority</strong></li>
<li><strong>responsibility</strong></li>
<li><strong>ownership</strong></li>
<li><strong>the way decisions are made</strong></li>
<li><strong>family relationships</strong></li>
<li><strong>the role of professional management</strong></li>
<li><strong>the identity of the business</strong></li>
<li><strong>the relationship with capital</strong></li>
<li><strong>the relationship with legacy.</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>And none of it changes on the same day. That is why a transition cannot have just a date. It can have a beginning. It can have phases. It can have key decisions. But there is no single moment at which we can say:</p>

<p><strong>"The transition is now complete."</strong></p>

<p>The real question is not:</p>

<p><strong>When will the founder leave?</strong></p>

<p>The real question is:</p>

<p><strong>When will the system become capable of operating without them?</strong></p>

<p>That is a far more demanding test. And it is exactly why a generational transition needs to be built long before it becomes urgent.</p>
